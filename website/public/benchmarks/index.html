Benchmarks: peer to peer across Europe

One device in Paris holds the files. Devices in Amsterdam and Warsaw join the vault with an empty folder and download everything: once directly from the Paris device and from each other (peer to peer), once from an S3 bucket in Paris. Measured on 9 and 10 October 2026 on rented cloud machines.

This page reports what was measured, how, what limited it, and what changed in Varsto because of it. Every planned case now has numbers, from two runs each unless a table says otherwise. The numbers belong to an alpha: they describe the code as it was on those days. The driver is in the repository under scripts/bench/, and the summarised results are published as JSON, so every number on this page can be checked and the run repeated.

Summary

  • Many small files, Paris to Amsterdam (9 ms apart): 10 000 files arrived peer to peer in 111 s (90 files/s), from the bucket in 299 s (34 files/s): peer to peer was 2.7 times faster. To Warsaw (33 ms apart) the two were close: 260 s against 294 s. The bucket's speed changed with the hour, though: three hours later, with two readers at once, it delivered 64 files/s to Amsterdam and 53 to Warsaw, close to peer to peer's 70 and 39 with two readers.
  • Large files: to Amsterdam, 4 GiB peer to peer in 159 s (27.0 MB/s) against 180 s (23.9 MB/s) from the bucket, and much steadier. To Warsaw the bucket was faster: 190 s (23.4 MB/s) against 423 s (10.2 MB/s). The peer-to-peer path opens a TCP connection for every block, which costs round trips; the longer the distance, the more.
  • Two readers at once, peer to peer: the readers help each other. Warsaw took 69 % of its blocks from Amsterdam, which had them already, and only 31 % from Paris, so the Paris device sent 5.6 GB instead of 8.6 GB. Neither reader slowed down.
  • Compressible text: the bucket was a little faster to Amsterdam (18.1 against 16.7 MB/s) and more than twice as fast to Warsaw (16.7 against 7.3 MB/s). A device that serves text compresses every block again for every request, at a level chosen for small storage, not speed.
  • Every block of the peer-to-peer runs came from a peer: 202 544 of 202 544 blocks in 24 measured downloads, none from the bucket, whose blocks had been deleted; every file matched its SHA-256 afterwards.
  • The runs found and fixed nine problems, among them downloads that fetched one block at a time (now eight in flight, and small files fetched ahead across files) and a peer-to-peer server that answered one request at a time. Making QUIC the preferred path across the internet was tried, measured slower here, and reverted. Details and numbers.

Two runs per case; times in the tables are their median (the mean of two), with both runs next to it. Single runs say so. Between the two days the download code changed only in ways that do not touch the compared case: the 9 October large-file and text runs fetch blocks inside a file exactly as the 10 October build does, and the many-small-files bucket runs were repeated on 10 October because fetching ahead across files is new. The last two cases, compressible text peer to peer and many small files from the bucket, each with both readers at once, were run three hours after the others on fresh machines with the merged build; the bucket was clearly faster in that session than in the earlier one.

Setup and baseline

  • Machines: three Scaleway POP2-4C-16G instances (4 vCPU AMD EPYC 7543, 16 GB RAM, 80 GB network block volume, Ubuntu 24.04), created fresh each day: the writer in Paris (fr-par-1), readers in Amsterdam (nl-ams-1) and Warsaw (pl-waw-1). Each machine had a public IPv4 address on its own interface, so there was no NAT between them.
  • Bucket: a temporary Scaleway Object Storage bucket in Paris (fr-par), the writer's region. The readers in Amsterdam and Warsaw read it across borders.
  • Varsto: 0.0.1-alpha.8 with the fixes listed below, a static Linux build (musl). 9 October: commits up to 37bee19 (bucket runs: 0180ca8, which differs only in the peer-to-peer server and the file watcher). 10 October: commit 9b93263 for the peer-to-peer runs; that day's earlier bucket runs used a3ba1c0, which differs only in the order of peer-to-peer routes; the two cases run last used the merged build dc09721, the same code as 9b93263 for these runs.
  • A run: a new device joins the vault with the vault key, starts the background service, pauses it, attaches an empty folder and asks the service for one sync of that folder (POST /api/sync). The time is from that request to its answer: the file list is read, every block is fetched, checked against its hash, decrypted, checked against the chunk id, and written. Afterwards the driver compares a SHA-256 list of the folder with the writer's. Then the device is removed again. Automatic verification was turned off on all benchmark devices, because it downloads and repairs blocks in the background.

The links between the machines carried 700 to 950 Mbit/s (90 to 120 MB/s) in one TCP stream, and none of the Varsto transfers came close: all were limited by round trips and per-request work. The bucket's own speed varied a lot from one day to the next (the second measurement was taken in the early morning); its runs varied more than the peer-to-peer runs throughout.

Data sets

  • Large files: two files of 2 GiB of random (incompressible) bytes, 4 GiB in all, about 13 400 blocks.
  • Many small files: 10 000 files in 500 directories, sizes drawn from a log-normal distribution with a median of 16 KiB, cut to 1 KiB…256 KiB, one file in twenty a copy of another; 281 MiB in all. A 50 000-file set of the same shape was pushed, not downloaded.
  • Compressible text: 128 files of 4 MiB of generated web-server log lines, 512 MiB in all; zstd stores them in 138 MiB.

What “peer to peer only” means

Varsto asks peers first and storages second, so a peer-to-peer measurement must make sure the bucket cannot help. After the writer had pushed and the bucket runs were done, every object under chunks/ was deleted from the bucket. The bucket kept what devices need to find each other and to know what exists (signed ledger, file lists, device and peer records), but no file content: a block that no peer could deliver would have left the file unavailable. Then:

  • each run's pull report counted blocks from peers and blocks in all, and the driver failed any run where the two differed;
  • the readers' traffic counters (/api/p2p/traffic) and the writer's agreed: with one reader, the reader received from writer-paris exactly the stored size of the data set and the writer sent the same; with two readers, what each received from Paris and from the other reader added up to the stored size, and the Paris device sent the rest;
  • the path was direct in every run, over TCP to the writer's public address on port 17893 (the service log names the route it settled on), and between the two readers in the both-at-once runs. No relay was involved; a LAN path was impossible between three countries.

To Amsterdam, nine milliseconds away, peer to peer beat the bucket by a little and was much steadier: every peer-to-peer run took 158 s to 162 s, the bucket's 171 s and 190 s. To Warsaw the bucket was more than twice as fast. Each block of about 300 KiB is one HTTP request on a new TCP connection: a handshake, then TCP slow start from ten packets, so a block needs several round trips however fast the link is. At 33 ms that caps eight requests in flight at about 10 MB/s, which is what was measured. The bucket's HTTPS connections are kept open between requests.

With both readers at once, Warsaw took 2.97 GB of its 4.30 GB from Amsterdam (20 ms away, closer than Paris) and only 1.3 GB from Paris; Amsterdam, which had the blocks first, served them while it downloaded. Warsaw was even a little faster than alone (11.8 against 10.2 MB/s) and Amsterdam as fast as alone. The bucket served two readers at once without slowing either, too (45 and 21 MB/s); it is shared infrastructure built for many readers.

The reader's own work: about 40 s of CPU time for 4 GiB (decrypting, two hashes per block, writing), at most 490 MB of memory. The Paris device used 40 s of CPU time to serve each 4 GiB, one core about a quarter busy.

To Amsterdam, peer to peer delivered 90 files a second, 2.7 times the bucket's 34. To Warsaw, three and a half times farther away, both came to the same 34 to 38 files a second: the round trip per request dominates there, on both paths. Fetching ahead across files doubled the bucket's speed to Warsaw (from 17 to 34 files a second) and helped to Amsterdam (26 to 34). Once the blocks are there, writing each file (data synced to disk, a rename, ledger events) costs a few milliseconds on these network block volumes, which caps any source at a few hundred files a second.

With both readers at once, Warsaw was as fast as alone and Amsterdam's two runs differed (202 s and 110 s): the readers' blocks are fetched from whichever peer answers first, and one run waited on the other reader more than the other run did. The bucket, measured with both readers three hours later, delivered 64 files a second to Amsterdam and 53 to Warsaw: nearly twice what it gave single readers in the earlier session. Its speed depends on the hour more than on the number of readers.

  • Text costs about 85 s of CPU per GiB: zstd at level 12, a format constant, is slow on compressible data; it buys the 3.7 : 1 ratio every later transfer profits from.

Tried and reverted: QUIC first

Because the TCP path opens a connection per block, QUIC looked like the cure: Varsto already speaks QUIC to peers behind NAT, with one connection per peer and one stream per request. On 10 October the route order was changed to try QUIC before TCP for addresses beyond the LAN. It was slower on these machines, and used more than twice the CPU time:

So TCP stays first and QUIC remains the way through NATs. Where the QUIC path loses its time was not found in the time available; the host dropped no UDP packets. The experiment also turned up a gap that is not fixed yet: a device's peer table is built before a sync reads the device registry, so a device that has just joined is refused over QUIC by its peers until their next sync (over TCP it is served at once).

Still slow, and why

  • One TCP connection per block on the peer-to-peer path. Each request pays a handshake and a fresh slow start, which is what limits Warsaw. Keeping connections open (HTTP keep-alive with a small pool per peer) is the next thing to try, now that QUIC did not help here.
  • Serving compressible data costs CPU, because each request compresses the block again. Keeping recently served objects, or the stored objects of a local disk storage, would avoid it.
  • Writing many small files costs a disk sync per file on the reader, and on the writer two syncs per file for the logical clock.
  • Uploads to a bucket go one block at a time, with a HEAD before every PUT.
  • The ledger grows by about 1 KB per block: pushing the 4 GiB set wrote a 13 MB ledger batch, which every device reads when it joins.

When peer to peer wins, and when the bucket does

Varsto uses both on purpose, and the measurements show why.

  • Peer to peer needs a peer online. The readers could fetch only because a device that had the blocks was running. The bucket is always there: a laptop that is closed, a phone in a drawer, devices that are never online at the same time all still sync through it. That is why a vault always has at least one storage, and why the file lists and the ledger stay in it.
  • Near devices, peer to peer wins: 2.7 times faster for many small files to Amsterdam than the bucket in the same session (in a later session the bucket came close), a little faster and much steadier for large files. On a LAN the round trip is a fraction of a millisecond.
  • Devices that already have the data share it. With two readers, Warsaw took most of its blocks from Amsterdam; the first device to sync becomes a source for the next, and the writer's upload is spared.
  • Far devices and compressible data, the bucket won on these links: large files and text to Warsaw, text to Amsterdam by a little. The causes are known (a connection per block, compressing again when serving) and are not in the network: the link to Warsaw carried 777 Mbit/s in one stream.
  • The bucket scales to many readers without anyone's upload; a home device serving many others is limited by its upload, unless the readers help each other as above.
  • Peer to peer costs nothing per gigabyte and leaves no copy with a provider. The data goes from your device to your device; the bucket needs the blocks only for the devices that are offline when others want them.

Cost

Scaleway's public price list (read on 9 October 2026, Paris region, before tax): Object Storage Standard Multi-AZ €0.000022 per GB-hour (about €0.016 per GB-month), requests included, ingress included, egress the first 75 GB a month free and then €0.01 per GB. Transfers between regions (Paris – Amsterdam, Paris – Warsaw) count as egress; within a region they are free.

So one reader in Amsterdam downloading the 4 GiB set from the Paris bucket costs about €0.04 once the free 75 GB are used, and a household that syncs 500 GB a month to devices outside the bucket's region pays about €4 a month in egress. Peer to peer moves the same bytes over the devices' own connections and adds nothing to the storage bill. Other providers charge more for egress or per request; the method above applies to them unchanged. The machines for this benchmark (three instances at €0.147 an hour, about seven and a half hours over the two days) cost about €3.50.

Limits of this benchmark

  • Two days, two builds. The large-file runs from single readers and part of the bucket runs are from 9 October, the rest from 10 October; the changes between them do not touch the compared cases (see above). The bucket's speed differed between the days.
  • Cloud machines, not homes. Data-centre links, public addresses on the interface, no NAT, no Wi-Fi. At home the peer-to-peer path across the internet is usually QUIC through a hole-punched NAT, which these runs measured only in the reverted experiment; home upload speed would often be the limit.
  • Three regions of one provider, in Europe. The bucket and the machines share a provider and its backbone; other providers and longer distances will differ.
  • Network block volumes make every disk sync slow (milliseconds), which weighs on small files; a laptop's SSD syncs faster.
  • Two runs per case are few; the bucket's results varied by up to 58 % between repetitions for small files and by a factor of 3.5 for text, and the bucket's small-file speed nearly doubled between two sessions on the same day.
  • Alpha software. Formats and defaults may still change, and so will these numbers.

Reproduce: scripts/bench/p2p-bench.py creates the machines and the bucket with the Scaleway command line, runs each step, writes results.jsonl, and deletes everything again; report.py and page.py turn the results into the summary and the tables on this page.
