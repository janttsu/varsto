Features

Varsto is built around one idea: your files live on devices and storage you choose, every copy is ciphertext, and the app keeps honest, signed bookkeeping of where every block is. The features below follow from that. Everything described here is in the released version, 0.0.1-alpha.14; the list at the end is what is built on the main branch after it, and what is designed but not built. The Use cases page tells the same story from the user's side, one situation at a time.

A ledger that counts every copy, and policies that warn in time

Every device keeps a signed, encrypted ledger of what it stored, verified and published, and every other device replays it through your storages. Varsto therefore knows how many copies of every block exist, where they are, and when another device last checked them. A policy like "two cloud copies and one at home, verified within 30 days" is evaluated from that ledger alone, and you are warned before it stops being true.

There is no server: a device writes encrypted blocks into storage you own and, next to them, its ledger; another device reads it when it reaches the same storage, verifies the signatures and knows what exists, where it is and which copies someone else has checked. Devices that are never online at the same time still converge. Every folder starts with a default policy (one copy on any storage, one in external storage, one on your own devices or disks, verified by another device within 30 days), and the policy travels with the folder. The app distinguishes ok, at risk (a verification is ageing out, or the only spare copy sits on cold storage or on a disk that is away), violated and unknown, and tells you while there is still time: a notification from the background app, a banner in the interface, and varsto policy check with an exit code for cron or monitoring. On the desktop, a "Where your data is" chart draws each folder flowing into the storages and devices that hold it, in proportion to the bytes, with how many copies every block has.

Verification is automatic: the background service on each device re-reads, within a daily budget, blocks that other devices wrote, checks their hash and records it in the ledger, oldest first and long before a window runs out; cold storage is never read for it. A copy found missing or damaged is repaired automatically from another storage, this device's own files or a peer, and the copies that cannot be repaired raise a notification with the reason (varsto repair). varsto fsck --verify compares the ledger with what the storages actually hold and re-hashes every object. A device restored from an old backup is detected and fenced. A rolling bit-rot scrub of every copy on this device's storages and disks, and of the synced files on the device, is built new in 0.0.1-alpha.14.

Your own storage: buckets, rclone remotes, cold storage, with the costs shown

A directory, a NAS share, an S3 bucket at any provider or any rclone remote becomes a storage; several at once if you like. Files are cut into blocks, compressed and encrypted on your device, so a storage ever sees only opaque objects and their sizes. There is no Varsto account, server or relay: if the project stopped tomorrow, your devices would keep syncing.

S3-compatible buckets work with your own access keys (AWS, Scaleway, Hetzner, Backblaze B2, Cloudflare R2, MinIO), and any rclone remote brings SFTP, WebDAV, Google Drive and the rest. Blocks follow the file size, and the blocks of small files are written together in packs: a folder of thousands of notes becomes a handful of objects on the storage instead of thousands, which saves requests and the minimum object charges some providers make. Secrets stay encrypted on your device; rclone keeps its own. Archive classes such as Deep Archive are written but never read without an explicit confirmation, and the app reports an object that must be restored first instead of retrying. Every storage can carry a price per GB-month, for egress and for restore, set by you or taken from the open price data when the provider, region and class are recognised, so the app shows what each storage and each folder costs a month.

A folder can be written to chosen storages or places only (varsto folder placement), and varsto move (or the "Move to" suggestion) moves its blocks, or those of its idle files, to another storage such as a cold one: copied, checked, recorded, then removed from the old storage, never below the folder's policy and never the last copy, with the change in the monthly bill shown first. Blocks kept only on cold storage are read only after you move them back with a confirmed cold read. Removing a storage copies the blocks that would fall below the folder's minimum elsewhere first; a budgeted background drain and storage quotas come new in 0.0.1-alpha.14.

Old disks on a shelf as counted, verified copies

Plug a disk into a dock, add it to a pool, and Varsto fills it with the data that has the fewest copies, tells you when it is safe to eject, and recognises it when it comes back. Disks in different places count toward "one copy at home, one elsewhere". A file whose only copy is on a disk on the shelf says so: attach disk data-01.

The pool works: add, fill within a reserve, check (sizes or a full re-hash, queued deletions applied), eject, retire; disks are recognised by their marker wherever they appear. Disk groups keep N copies on disks kept in different places (each disk has a place; disk check fills what a place lacks, policies count each disk copy at its place and report blocks short of the rule), and a folder written only to a pool is a "disks only" folder with no cloud copy at all. Preparing a whole drive from the app (disk init), fetch plans that compare a disk from the shelf with the download, and suggestions of which disk to dock next are built new in 0.0.1-alpha.14.

Post-quantum hybrid cryptography for every stored signature and every sealed key

Every ledger batch is signed with Ed25519 and ML-DSA-65 (FIPS 204), and both signatures must verify. Folder keys travel to other devices and people sealed to an X25519 + ML-KEM-768 (FIPS 203) key, so a token can cross any channel. Algorithm identifiers in every object let the scheme change later without rewriting your data. Designed for a post-quantum world; not audited yet.

A key that has a post-quantum part never accepts an Ed25519-only signature, so nobody can strip the stronger half. Devices created with alpha-0 keep their Ed25519-only keys and stay valid through the algorithm identifier they carry; re-enrol such a device to give it a post-quantum key. Transport (direct connections carry only encrypted blocks with a per-request token; QUIC with TLS 1.3 and pinned certificates through NAT) and release signing (minisign) still use classical algorithms, and nothing has been audited yet.

Strongroom: folders that open only with your security key

A Strongroom folder's key is wrapped under a secret that only your FIDO2 security key produces after a touch (the hmac-secret extension); it is never at rest anywhere, not even under your vault key. Enforcement is cryptographic: no passphrase, stolen laptop or assistant can open it. Enrol a backup key, unlock for a window, work, lock.

While locked the folder is skipped by sync, cannot be shared, and is invisible to assistants. A backup key, say a second YubiKey kept in a safe, wraps the same folder key, and either one opens it. An existing folder can become a Strongroom: it gets a new key, everything is re-encrypted and uploaded again, the old copies are deleted from your storages once the new ones are in place, and your other devices swap their plain copies for placeholders on their next sync.

Needs the libfido2 tools (fido2-cred, fido2-assert); a software key exists for trying the flow. Touching the key happens on the command line (varsto strongroom convert, add-key, rekey); the interface shows the command and lists the enrolled keys. A device that already had the old key could have kept it; removing a key does not re-key the folder, varsto strongroom rekey does.

Devices that help each other: peer to peer, and transferrer disks

Your devices exchange encrypted blocks directly: on the LAN automatically, across the internet through NAT with hole punching, and through one of your own reachable devices when punching fails; there is no vendor relay. A USB stick can act as a transferrer that carries only what the other side lacks, and a friend's NAS can hold verified copies it cannot read.

Peer-to-peer is on by default (port 17893; varsto p2p disable turns it off). A new file is announced before its upload, so your devices on the same network fetch it while the copy to the storages is still on its way. Every device that has a file can serve its encrypted blocks, byte for byte the same objects the storages hold; a device that needs a block asks its peers first and the cloud second, checks every block against its content hash, and spreads the work over the peers that answer. Across the internet devices learn their public address from STUN, punch through NAT over QUIC with a certificate pinned per device, and fall back to one of your own reachable devices (a home server, a forwarded port, a VPS), which sees only ciphertext. A live traffic view on the Peers page (varsto p2p traffic --watch) shows which device is sending to which, over which path and how fast. Blocks are compressed before encryption (zstd), so every transfer and every copy is small. Measured across three countries and from a home behind NAT: Benchmarks.

A transferrer is a USB stick or an external disk marked --carrier on both machines. It receives only the encrypted blocks the other device still lacks, carries the ledger batches as well, and once both ends hold a block it is removed from the stick. For gigabytes of video between home and office this is faster than any upload, and it works with no internet at all.

NAT traversal is tested on localhost, on the maintainer's own networks and on a three-device rig across NATs; two symmetric NATs cannot be punched and need one reachable device in the vault, and sockets are IPv4 only. Router port mapping (PCP, NAT-PMP, UPnP) is built new in 0.0.1-alpha.14.

Untrusted replicas, and sharing a folder with another Varsto user

Give a friend, a relative or a rented box a replica token. Their device copies your encrypted objects from a shared location to its own disk, verifies every hash, and reports the copies into your ledger. It never receives a key that could open anything; it learns object names and sizes, exactly what a storage provider learns. Two users can host each other's backups this way, and both see in their own app that the copies exist and are verified.

Sharing is key sharing: the recipient's device prints a request code, the owner seals the folder key to it (hybrid X25519 + ML-KEM-768) and sends the token back over any channel; only the requesting device can open it. The recipient syncs the folder through storage both can reach, and their device writes its own signed ledger under the folder key, so the owner sees their copies too. The token opens that folder and nothing else of the vault. Before sealing, you and the recipient compare six words derived from the request code, over a call or in person, so a code swapped on its way is caught. The Shared page lists who has access, with the words you confirmed; Remove… gives the folder a new key that only you and the members who stay receive, and a token nobody accepted yet can be revoked the same way.

varsto replica token, replica init and replica run; varsto share create and share accept, or the same buttons in the interface. QR invitations and varsto:// links are built new in 0.0.1-alpha.14.

Backups: encrypted off-site snapshots that nothing can change

Pick any folder on any device, say your documents, a project or a server's data directory, and Varsto backs it up to your external storages: an S3 bucket, an rclone remote, a NAS or cold storage. Each backup is a read-only snapshot, encrypted exactly like everything else Varsto keeps, and it lives only on those storages, never on your other devices. Snapshots are taken daily, weekly or when you ask. Each one is kept for the number of days you choose, 90 by default, and is deleted from storage once it expires. Blocks that did not change are stored once, however many snapshots use them.

Nothing edits a snapshot after it is taken. A restore always goes into a directory you name, from the app's Backups page or with varsto backup restore, and you can pick the newest snapshot, a numbered one or the one from a given date.

Use case: a small office backs up the accounting folder of one PC and a shared drive of another to a bucket at a second provider every night. Three months of history stay available, ransomware on the PCs cannot rewrite them, and nobody has to remember to plug in a disk.

Organizations: administrators, approvals and a signed log, for business use

Use case. A small firm keeps its project folders in its own bucket. The office manager is the administrator: a new employee installs Varsto, sends a request code, and the manager compares six words with them on the phone and approves the laptop. When someone leaves, one action removes them and every device of theirs, and the remaining devices get new keys.

A vault has devices, not people, and every full device is equal. An organization adds the layer a company needs: administrators who approve devices for named people, remove a person with every device of theirs in one step (the vault gets new keys that only the remaining devices receive; with a wipe order the removed devices delete their keys and folder contents), set what members may do on their own (share outside the vault, add storages, set policies, add devices), and keep a log of it that is signed by their devices and chained, so nobody can change it afterwards unnoticed. A device joins with a request code and a six-word fingerprint the administrator compares with the person; the approval token, sealed to that device's key, carries the vault key and the storage settings. A device that joined with the vault key alone is ignored by the others until an administrator adds it. The root key that appoints administrators is 24 words on paper, never on a device.

Getting started. Use with a team under Settings, or varsto org create --name … --user …, turns a vault into an organization; the devices it already has become the founder's devices, and the device that creates it is the first administrator. The Organization page lists people and their devices, approves new ones, sets what members may do, appoints or dismisses administrators with the 24 root words, and shows the signed log (exportable as JSON); members see the page without the administrator actions. Only administrators can remove devices, and peers refuse devices that are not in the roster.

A vault without an organization is unchanged: no new objects, no new checks, nothing in the interface beyond the Use with a team card in Settings. Storage credentials are still shared and not rotated, existing data is not re-encrypted after a removal, and there is no second factor on administrator actions yet.

An MCP server: let your assistant read, analyse and tidy, with exactly the access you grant

varsto mcp speaks the Model Context Protocol, so a language model running on your own machine (through Ollama, LM Studio or any MCP client that runs locally) can work with your files without anything leaving your devices: read and summarise them, find what belongs together, propose a tidier structure, move and rename files into it, and leave an index or notes, all inside the folders you granted and read-only unless you said otherwise. Strongroom folders are never visible, and everything the assistant writes syncs like any other change. It can never delete or overwrite a file: there is no delete tool, it only creates new files, and moves and renames go only to free names.

Varsto itself keeps the last-used record of every file, merged across your devices so a file counts as idle only if no device used it, and suggests where idle data would be cheapest to keep; the assistant can see that estimate for context, but placement advice is Varsto's own job, not the assistant's.

A recovery kit for paper or metal, and removing a lost device

There is no account and no escrow: the vault key is yours alone, so the app helps you keep it. varsto recovery kit prints the key as 24 words with a checksum, laid out for paper or metal, and optionally three shares of which any two rebuild the key and one alone reveals nothing, so no single place has to hold everything. A new device joins straight from the words. Test it once a year.

From any other device, Remove device signs a revocation into your storage. Every device then ignores the removed one's ledger entries after the cut-off and refuses its peer connections, and the vault moves to a new key that is sealed (X25519 + ML-KEM-768) to each remaining device only: ledger, records, manifests and new files of every folder use keys the removed device never sees. With a wipe order, the device deletes its keys, sync state and folder contents the next time it runs Varsto and reaches one of your storages; the order is signed by one of your devices, so a storage or someone with an old vault key cannot trigger it.

What the removed device could read before stays readable to it; folders shared with other people and Strongroom folders keep their keys; storage credentials are not rotated for you. Print a new recovery kit afterwards.

A background app on every desktop, selective sync with placeholders, and a phone that keeps nothing in clear text

The background service starts at login (a systemd user unit on Linux, a launch agent on macOS, the tray app on Windows) and is set up by the first command once a vault exists. It watches your folders and syncs changes within seconds, with a periodic catch-up for changes made elsewhere; there are no sync, refresh or check buttons to press. The same small binary provides the command line and the local interface, served only to your computer. Updates are looked for automatically and offered only when a newer version exists; installing one checks the release signature and the GitHub checksums, and the macOS app replaces itself from the signed disk image. varsto transfers --watch shows the uploads, downloads and peer traffic running now.

Every device can keep a folder in selective mode: the full tree is visible as placeholders, a file is downloaded when you ask for it, and Free up space turns it back into a placeholder once it is safely stored elsewhere. Placeholders are never mistaken for deletions. Right-click Download and Free up space work in Finder, Explorer and the common Linux file managers, and double-clicking a placeholder opens the file; native placeholders (File Provider, Cloud Files) come later. Thumbnails are generated once, encrypted under the folder key and shared with your other devices, so a phone shows previews of pictures it never downloaded.

Android runs the same encrypted core; the iOS shell builds and runs in the Simulator only. The Android alpha is a sideloadable APK with a compact file list (a long press selects files to download or free). A folder kept encrypted on the phone holds only encrypted blocks there, the same ones your storages hold; the app shows pictures, video (with seeking), audio and text from such folders in its own viewer, decrypted in memory and never written to the phone, while PDFs and other files open in another app, which receives a decrypted copy that Varsto removes when it locks or restarts. Camera upload sends new photos and videos to a folder in YYYY/MM subfolders, optionally only on Wi-Fi or while charging. iOS has neither yet and has not run on a real device.

After 0.0.1-alpha.14

Built on the main branch new in 0.0.1-alpha.14. The storage format describes each of them in full.

  • A vault-wide trash: a deleted file keeps its last version, restorable on any device for the trash days.
  • Version history of files, with the days of history you choose.
  • A second, independent backup with restic into a repository outside your storages, restorable with restic alone.
  • Imports from git-annex repositories (disk by disk, naming the disk to insert next), from cloud drives through rclone, and from local directories, every file checked against its hash.
  • A rolling bit-rot scrub of every copy on your storages and attached disks and of the synced files on the device, with repair from another copy.
  • QR invitations and varsto:// links for sharing and pairing.
  • Draining a storage within a daily budget, and storage quotas.
  • Router port mapping (PCP, NAT-PMP, UPnP) for peer-to-peer.
  • Folder safety: a folder marker, a mass-deletion guard, send-only and receive-only folders.
  • Ignore rules (.varstoignore, shared or device-local).
  • File metadata: permissions, symbolic links, extended attributes and ownership.
  • Preparing whole drives for disk pools, fetch plans and the next disk to dock; live activity between devices; redacted local debug logs (encryption at rest later).

Also in the design

  • Per-device storage credentials, rotated at the provider when a device is removed.
  • Re-encrypting existing data after a device is removed.
  • Incremental manifests.
  • DHT peer discovery.
  • Proof-of-storage challenges.
  • Erasure coding across storages.
  • A File Provider / Cloud Files integration for placeholders.
  • iOS camera upload and viewer.
  • A second factor for device enrolment.

Every design document on the Docs page says which parts exist. Nothing has been audited yet.
